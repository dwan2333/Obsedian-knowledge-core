# When the System Breaks

*Part 7 of 7 · [Series overview](<Treasury Securities and the Interest Rate System (Main).md>)*

---

Every crisis follows the same path: lenders lose confidence and stop rolling overnight loans, borrowers are forced to sell, prices fall, more margin calls follow, and it ends only when the Federal Reserve or the government steps in.

---

## 2008: the run on repurchase agreements

Investment banks such as Bear Stearns and Lehman Brothers were dealers holding huge piles of mortgage securities, financed overnight with small haircuts, often at 30 to 1 leverage or more. The collateral that failed was mortgage securities, not Treasury securities: Treasury-backed lending kept working, and Treasury securities became the safe haven.

| When                    | What happened                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        |
| ----------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| June 2007               | Two Bear Stearns hedge funds full of subprime mortgage securities missed margin calls. A lender, Merrill Lynch, seized hundreds of millions of dollars of their collateral and found almost no buyers near its stated value                                          |
| Late 2007 to early 2008 | Lenders raised haircuts on mortgage collateral from a few percent to 50% or more, or refused it. Dealers and hedge funds sold assets to raise cash, pushing prices lower                                                                                             |
| March 2008              | Lenders stopped rolling Bear Stearns's loans, and its cash ran out within days. The Federal Reserve helped JPMorgan Chase buy it, taking on about &#36;29 billion of its riskiest assets, and created the Primary Dealer Credit Facility to lend to dealers directly |
| September 15, 2008      | Lehman Brothers, with about &#36;639 billion in assets, filed the largest bankruptcy in United States history                                                                                                                                                        |
| September 16, 2008      | The Reserve Primary Fund, holding about &#36;785 million of Lehman's short-term debt, fell to &#36;0.97 a share ("breaking the buck"). Savers pulled hundreds of billions from money funds that week, and money funds stopped lending                                |

| Rescue                                                                        | What it did                                                                                    |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Treasury guarantee of money market funds (September 19)                       | Stopped the run on money funds                                                                 |
| Federal Reserve lending programs                                              | Bought short-term debt from money funds and lent to dealers against a wide range of collateral |
| Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley became bank holding companies (September 21) | Gave them permanent access to Federal Reserve lending                                          |
| Troubled Asset Relief Program (October 3)                                     | &#36;700 billion to put capital into banks                                                     |

## March 2020: when Treasury lending itself broke

1. **A rush for cash:** as COVID hit, investors and foreign governments sold Treasury securities to raise dollars.
2. **Calm prices turned volatile**, and hedge funds faced large margin calls on their futures and borrowed positions.
3. **Basis trades unwound:** hedge funds sold Treasury securities to raise cash.
4. **Dealers could not absorb it:** their balance sheets were full and capital rules capped them.
5. **The market seized up:** even the world's safest asset became hard to sell at fair prices.

The Federal Reserve bought roughly a trillion dollars of Treasury securities in about a month, offered enormous overnight lending, and temporarily stopped counting Treasury securities and reserves toward big banks' leverage rules, freeing up exactly the balance-sheet space dealers had run out of.

---

## 1998: the original hedge fund collapse

Long-Term Capital Management ran leveraged bond trades at roughly 25 to 1. When Russia defaulted in August 1998, its trades collapsed. Because so many dealers had lent to it, the Federal Reserve Bank of New York organized 14 banks to put in about &#36;3.6 billion and wind it down in an orderly way, with no taxpayer money.

---

## What changed afterward

| Lesson | What changed |
|---|---|
| Money funds can suffer runs | Stricter rules on what money funds hold and how they handle withdrawals |
| Banks ran out of cash in days | Liquidity rules requiring big banks to hold 30 days of safe assets |
| Dealers were too leveraged | Leverage rules based on balance-sheet size |
| No one knew who was exposed to whom | Central clearing, mandatory for repurchase agreements by June 30, 2027 |
| The Federal Reserve had to improvise | The standing repurchase agreement facility (2021), now a permanent 4.00% ceiling |
