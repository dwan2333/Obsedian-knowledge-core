Practice problem · linear ↔ log transformation

The Petri Dish Mystery

A microbiologist tracks a bacterial colony over time. Each part below tests a different skill: recognizing exponential structure, computing the growth rate, choosing the right axis, extrapolating a trend, and most importantly — detecting when growth deviates from the prediction. Try each part on your own first; then reveal the solution.

— The scenario
A bacterial colony, observed every 3 hours

At noon, a microbiologist seeds a petri dish with 100 bacteria. She measures the population every 3 hours and gets the following counts. Most rows fit an obvious pattern — but the last reading at t = 18 hours doesn't fit. The questions below will walk you through extracting the pattern, predicting future values, and understanding what the anomaly means.

a. Looking only at the data from t = 0 through t = 15, does this look like exponential growth? If so, what's the doubling time?

Yes — each measurement is exactly double the previous one.

100 → 200 → 400 → 800 → 1600 → 3200
each step is ×2, and each step covers 3 hours

So the doubling time is exactly 3 hours.

How to spot exponential structure in a table: divide each value by the previous. If the ratio is constant across rows, the data is exponential. Constant ratio → exponential. (Constant difference would mean linear.)
b. What's the hourly growth factor r — the number you multiply by every single hour? And what does that say about the percent growth per hour?

If r is the hourly multiplier, then after the doubling time Td = 3 hours the colony has multiplied by 2:

r3 = 2    ⟹    r = 21/3 ≈ 1.2599

So the colony grows by about 26% per hour. You can verify: 100 × 1.2599 ≈ 126, then × 1.2599 ≈ 159, then × 1.2599 ≈ 200 ✓ — three hours of 26% compounding gives a doubling.

The relationship r = 21/T_d is worth memorizing. Equivalently: the doubling time and the hourly growth rate carry exactly the same information — choose whichever is more intuitive to talk about. "Doubles every 3 hours" and "grows 26% per hour" describe the same process.
c. If you plot this data on a linear y-axis, what shape does it take? On a log10 y-axis, what shape? What's the slope of the log-axis line?

Linear axis: the early data points are tightly squashed near the x-axis (100, 200, 400 are visually almost indistinguishable on a y-axis that has to reach 3,200). The curve looks nearly flat for the first ~9 hours, then sweeps sharply upward. This is the familiar "hockey stick" of exponential growth on linear axes.

Log axis: a perfectly straight line. Each 3-hour step moves up by the same vertical distance — namely log10(2) ≈ 0.301 — because each step represents a multiplication by 2.

slope (per hour) = log10(r) = log10(21/3) = (1/3) · log10(2) ≈ 0.1003
i.e., y rises by 0.1003 on the log scale for each 1 hour of x

Equivalently: it takes about 1 / 0.1003 ≈ 9.97 hours to climb one decade (×10) on the log axis. So every ~10 hours, the population multiplies by 10.

The slope IS the growth rate. Once you've plotted exponential data on a log axis and confirmed it's straight, the slope of the line directly encodes how fast the process multiplies. Steeper slope → faster doubling. No calculator needed if you have a ruler.
d. Assume the doubling-every-3-hours pattern continues. When will the population first reach 1 million bacteria?

We need to find t such that 100 × 2t/3 = 1,000,000. Divide both sides by 100:

2t/3 = 10,000
(t/3) · log10(2) = log10(10,000) = 4
t/3 = 4 / 0.301 ≈ 13.29
t ≈ 39.9 hours

Roughly 40 hours — or about a day and a half after noon.

Sanity check by counting doublings: 100 → 1,000,000 is a factor of 10,000 = 213.29. We need 13.29 doublings, each taking 3 hours, totaling 39.87 hours. ✓

The "decade method" is fastest: 100 → 1,000,000 is 4 decades (×10⁴). Each decade takes ~10 hours (from part c). So 4 × 10 = 40 hours. This is the kind of mental arithmetic that log axes make easy — exactly why epidemiologists used them in 2020 to project when case counts would cross critical thresholds.
e. The pure-exponential model predicts 6,400 at t = 18, but the actual measurement is 5,400. What does this tell the microbiologist? Which axis — linear or log — would make this deviation more visible?

What the anomaly tells her: The colony is no longer growing at a constant 26%/hour. Something is slowing growth — limited nutrients, accumulated waste products, space constraints, antibiotic activity. In ecology this is called the logistic transition: when resources are abundant, growth looks exponential; once resources become scarce, the curve starts bending.

How big is the deviation? 5,400 / 6,400 ≈ 0.844, so the population is about 16% below the exponential trajectory. Small in absolute terms — only 1,000 bacteria — but a clear deviation from the pattern.

On a linear axis: 5,400 vs 6,400 is a tiny gap when the y-axis has to span all the way from 100 to 6,400+. The eye can barely tell the data point is below where it "should" be. The deviation is visually invisible.

On a log axis: the perfect straight line through t = 0 to t = 15 suddenly bends downward at t = 18. The bending is immediately obvious — your eye is excellent at spotting deviations from a straight line. Even a 16% deviation is conspicuous because the line was so cleanly straight before it.

This is the #1 real-world use case for log axes. Detecting changes in trend — is the growth still accelerating at the same rate, slowing down, or accelerating further? — is extremely hard on linear axes (everything that's growing looks like "going up") and easy on log axes (anything that deviates from a straight line is obvious). This is exactly why public-health professionals plotted COVID cases on log scales in March 2020: they needed to see whether the curve was bending, and that's only visible when the unbent version is a straight line.
— Five key takeaways

What this problem trained

  1. Constant ratio → exponential. If consecutive values in a table all differ by the same multiplicative factor, you have exponential growth. Constant difference would mean linear growth. The eye can spot constant ratios easily once you start dividing.
  2. Doubling time and hourly rate carry the same information. Use r = 21/T_d to convert between them. "Doubles every 3 hours" ↔ "grows 26%/hour" — pick whichever your audience finds intuitive.
  3. On a log axis, the slope IS the growth rate. Once exponential data plots as a straight line, the slope gives log r per unit time. Steeper line → faster doubling. No formula needed once you have the line.
  4. Use decade-counting for extrapolation. Going from 100 to 1,000,000 is 4 decades. If one decade takes 10 hours, four decades take 40 hours. This is way faster than solving exponential equations by hand.
  5. Log axes reveal trend changes invisible on linear ones. A bend in a previously straight line jumps out at the eye. The same data on a linear chart hides the deviation under the dominant exponential shape. This is the real superpower — not just visualizing exponentials, but detecting when they stop being exponential.
