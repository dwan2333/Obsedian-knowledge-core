Exactly one empty box

Throw n distinguishable balls into n distinguishable boxes, all placements equally likely. Watch the winning shape, build the count, and see the famous Stars-and-Bars trap that makes the "obvious" indistinguishable-balls formula give the wrong answer.

The setup

n — balls = boxes 3

Total possible arrangements: 27  =  nn with each ball independently choosing 1 of n boxes.

The winning shape

"Exactly one empty box" forces one box to hold 2 balls, every other box to hold 1 ball, and one box to hold 0 balls. This is the only shape that fits — by the pigeonhole principle, n balls in n−1 occupied boxes means one box must be doubled.

A random arrangement matching the shape. Click New random winning arrangement above to shuffle.

Building the count, step by step

Construct every possible winning arrangement by 4 independent choices, then multiply.

Step A
Pick the empty box (1 of n).
n = 3
Step B
Pick the crowded box (holds 2 balls).
n−1 = 2
Step C
Pick which 2 balls get paired in the crowded box.
C(n, 2) = 3
Step D
Place the remaining n−2 balls into the n−2 remaining boxes (1 each).
(n−2)! = 1
n · (n−1) · C(n, 2) · (n−2)!  =  n! · C(n, 2)  =  18 winning arrangements

Two probabilities — only one is right

✓ Correct — distinguishable balls
P = n! · C(n, 2) / nn
18 / 27  =  0.667
Every physical arrangement of n distinct balls is equally likely.
✗ Wrong — Stars-and-Bars trap
P = n(n−1) / C(2n−1, n)
6 / 10  =  0.600
Treats balls as identical — outcomes are not equally likely!

Why the Stars-and-Bars trap fails

The 2-ball, 2-box proof

Take n = 2. With distinguishable Ball A and Ball B, there are 4 physically distinct, equally likely arrangements:

ArrangementBox 1Box 2Empty box?
1A, B(empty)Yes — Box 2 ✓
2(empty)A, BYes — Box 1 ✓
3ABNo
4BANo

True probability: 2 / 4 = 1/2.

Now Stars and Bars treats balls as identical. It can't tell rows 3 and 4 apart ("just one ball in each box"), so it merges them into one outcome and reports a sample space of only 3:

Stars-and-Bars viewBox 1Box 2Empty?
1● ●(empty)Yes ✓
2(empty)● ●Yes ✓
3●●No (but secretly TWO physical realities!)

Stars and Bars now reports 2 / 3 ≈ 0.667 — wrong.

The fundamental error: By compressing two physical realities (A in Box 1 + B in Box 2; A in Box 2 + B in Box 1) into one "outcome," Stars and Bars makes its sample space uneven. The "one ball each" outcome is secretly twice as likely as either "both in one box" outcome — but the math treats them as having equal probability, and the answer drifts.

Golden rule: When computing physical-event probabilities (balls, dice, coins), always treat the items as distinct. Stars and Bars is for counting partitions, not for computing physical probabilities, because its outcomes are never uniformly distributed.

The big picture

The interactive at the top lets you change n and see how the formula scales. Watch the gap between the correct and wrong probabilities as n grows — the trap doesn't fix itself with larger samples.

A quick mental check at the next-up case (n = 3): distinguishable counting gives 18/27 ≈ 0.667; Stars and Bars gives 6/10 = 0.600. Different! And both are off from each other in every n.

Why does Stars and Bars exist at all then? It's perfect for counting distinct partitions, where you genuinely don't care about labels — e.g., "how many ways can 10 identical candies be split among 4 children" if children's labels are fixed but the candies are identical. It's a counting tool, not a probability tool. The mistake is using it as if every partition is equally likely.
