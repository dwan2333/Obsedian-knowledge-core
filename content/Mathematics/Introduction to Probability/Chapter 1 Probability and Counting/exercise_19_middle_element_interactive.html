Exercise 19 · Story Proof

5-Subsets via middle

∑k=2n (k⁄2) (n−k+2⁄2) = (n+3⁄5)
— prove for all integers n ≥ 2 —

The story

Both sides count the same thing: the number of 5-element subsets of {1, 2, ..., n+3}. The right-hand side counts directly. The left-hand side counts by sorting subsets according to their middle element (the 3rd smallest).

If the middle element of a 5-subset is m, then the subset has:

  • — 2 elements below m, chosen from {1, ..., m−1}  →  C(m−1, 2) ways
  • — 2 elements above m, chosen from {m+1, ..., n+3}  →  C(n+3−m, 2) ways

Setting k = m − 1 turns this into the LHS: each k from 2 to n picks out a distinct middle element m = k+1, and counts exactly the subsets having that middle. Sum over all valid middles, and you get every 5-subset once.

total =  ∑k=2..n  C(k, 2) · C(n+2−k, 2)  =  C(n+3, 5)

— Why this works

Every 5-element subset has exactly one middle element (the 3rd smallest after sorting). So when we group all 5-subsets by their middle, every subset lands in exactly one group, and the groups don't overlap. Adding up the group sizes gives the total number of 5-subsets.

The bijection k = m − 1 shifts the index purely for algebraic convenience — it turns "elements below m" from a count of m−1 into a count of k, which makes the binomial coefficients in the LHS look symmetrical. Either form counts the same thing.

This kind of argument — counting one thing two ways — is called a combinatorial identity by double counting, or in Blitzstein's vocabulary, a story proof. The formal manipulation of factorials would also work, but it would obscure why the identity holds. The story makes the equality feel inevitable.
