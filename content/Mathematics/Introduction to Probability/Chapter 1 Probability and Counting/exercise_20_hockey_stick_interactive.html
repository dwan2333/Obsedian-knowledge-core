The Hockey Stick

(k⁄k) + (k+1⁄k) + (k+2⁄k) + ⋯ + (n⁄k) = (n+1⁄k+1)
— for all positive integers n ≥ k —

The story

Both sides count the same thing: the number of (k+1)-element committees we can form from n+1 people, lined up by age from youngest (1) to oldest (n+1). The RHS counts these directly: C(n+1, k+1).

The LHS partitions the committees by their oldest member. If the oldest person on the committee is person j (where j ranges from k+1 up to n+1, since you need at least k younger people to fill out the committee), then the remaining k members are chosen from the j−1 people younger than j. That's C(j−1, k) ways.

Letting j run from k+1 to n+1, the LHS exponents (j−1) run from k to n — exactly the identity:

∑j=k+1..n+1 C(j−1, k)  =  C(n+1, k+1)

Every committee has exactly one oldest member, so summing across all possible oldest-ages counts each committee once. The two sides agree.

— Why the name

If you highlight the LHS terms in Pascal's triangle, they trace a diagonal line going down-and-to-the-right — the handle of the hockey stick. The RHS term sits one row below the bottom of that handle, offset to the right — the blade. The identity says: the handle's sum equals the blade's value.

This is a special case of a more general phenomenon: every entry in Pascal's triangle equals the sum of the entries on the diagonal above and to its left, terminating at the edge. It falls out of repeatedly applying Pascal's rule: C(n+1, k+1) = C(n, k) + C(n, k+1), and then expanding the second term the same way. Try the algebraic version on paper — it's a satisfying telescope.

Worth noticing: this is the discrete analogue of integration. The RHS C(n+1, k+1) is essentially a "running total" of the LHS terms C(j, k) for increasing j — the same structural pattern as ∫xᵏ dx = xᵏ⁺¹/(k+1). Binomial coefficients have a deep parallel with polynomials, and identities like this one are where the parallel really shows.
