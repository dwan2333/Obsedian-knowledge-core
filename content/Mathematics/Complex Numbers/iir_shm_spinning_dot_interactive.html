What you're watching

The complex equation z(t) = z(0)·e−iωt says the state vector rotates clockwise around the origin at angular speed ω. The starting position z(0) = A·e−iφ encodes two facts: its distance from the origin is A (the amplitude), and its starting angle is φ (the phase).

Now project that rotating point straight down onto the real axis — the "shadow" of the orbit. As the dot circles, its shadow slides back and forth between +A and −A, tracing out the cosine wave you see on the right. The horizontal coordinate at any time t is exactly A·cos(ωt + φ).

The bridge to physics. The same equation governs a mass on a spring. The spring's actual position x(t) is the shadow; the imaginary part of z(t) encodes its velocity (specifically, Im[z(t)] = −v(t)/ω). So watching the spring is watching the shadow of a circle you never see — and that's why the wave is sinusoidal in the first place.

How the physical inputs map to the math. If you start with position x0 and velocity v0, then

  • A = √(x02 + (v0/ω)2) — total "energy", combining stretch and kick
  • φ = −atan2(v0/ω, x0) — phase from the polar angle of the initial state in the complex plane

Special cases worth trying:

  • x0 = 1, v0 = 0 → pure cosine starting at peak (A = 1, φ = 0).
  • x0 = 0, v0 = 1 → pure sine starting at zero moving outward (A = 1/ω, φ = −π/2).
  • x0 = 1, v0 = 1, ω = 1 → a stretched-and-kicked combo: A = √2, φ = −π/4.
